## Why Four Scenarios Instead of One Number

The single easiest way to make a Bitcoin projection tool misleading is to present one confident growth rate as if it were a known fact. Nobody actually knows what Bitcoin's compound annual growth rate will be over the next 5 or 10 years — not analysts, not long-time holders, not this calculator. Presenting four clearly labeled scenarios, spanning a conservative-to-aggressive range, is a deliberate design choice meant to make that uncertainty visible rather than hiding it behind a single polished output.

## Bear: 15% CAGR

The Bear scenario represents a meaningfully below-historical-average growth assumption — roughly in the range of what a diversified, lower-volatility equity portfolio might be expected to return over the very long run, applied here to Bitcoin specifically as a conservative floor. This scenario is useful precisely because it's the least exciting number on the list: if an opportunity-cost comparison still looks compelling under the Bear case, that's a more robust signal than a comparison that only looks good under Hyperbull assumptions.

## Base: 21% CAGR

The Base scenario sits at a moderate assumption, intended as a reasonable "if you had to pick one number" default rather than either extreme. It's deliberately set below Bitcoin's actual historical long-run CAGR (which, measured from its earliest years, is dramatically higher than 21% — though largely because of how small and easy to multiply its earliest-year base was). Treating the Base scenario as a moderate, somewhat conservative-relative-to-history default, rather than as "the most likely outcome," is the more defensible way to interpret it.

## Bull: 35% CAGR

The Bull scenario reflects a more optimistic assumption, closer to the lower end of some multi-year historical stretches Bitcoin has actually produced, excluding its very earliest and most extreme early years. This is a reasonable scenario for someone who believes continued institutional adoption, growing scarcity narrative, and sustained demand growth will produce returns well above a conservative benchmark, without assuming a return to the most extreme historical growth rates from Bitcoin's early, tiny-market-cap years.

## Hyperbull: 55% CAGR

The Hyperbull scenario represents an aggressive assumption, intended to reflect some of Bitcoin's more favorable historical multi-year stretches rather than an average across its full history. This scenario should be treated as an upper bound for planning purposes — a "if conditions remain unusually favorable" case — not as an expectation. Anchoring financial decisions on the Hyperbull scenario specifically is the single most common way people over-extrapolate from a tool like this one.

## How These Numbers Were Chosen

These four values are fixed, deliberately round reference points spanning a conservative-to-aggressive range — they are not a statistical model fit to historical data, a professional forecast, or an endorsement of any specific outcome. They exist to give you four internally consistent points of comparison, not to claim precision that doesn't exist. If you have your own view on an appropriate growth rate — whether from your own research, a different data source, or simply a different risk tolerance — the slider underlying these four presets accepts any custom value from 5% to 60%, and using your own number is entirely reasonable and, in many cases, more appropriate than defaulting to one of the four presets.

## The Right Way to Use Four Scenarios

The value of seeing four outcomes instead of one isn't picking whichever one you find most emotionally satisfying — it's seeing the *range*. A $10,000 opportunity-cost projection that shows $14,000 under Bear and $23,000 under Hyperbull tells you two important things: the comparison is directionally favorable even under a pessimistic assumption, and the specific dollar figure you land on 10 years out is genuinely uncertain by a wide margin. Both of those facts are useful. Neither is useful in isolation — a comparison that only looks at Hyperbull is overconfident, and a comparison that only looks at Bear is unnecessarily pessimistic given what's historically been observed.

## A Practical Selection Framework

- **If you're skeptical of Bitcoin's long-term prospects but still curious about the comparison**: anchor on Bear, and treat anything above that as upside you're not counting on.
- **If you have no strong view either way**: Base is a reasonable, moderate default — but don't mistake it for a forecast.
- **If you're already a long-term holder with high conviction**: Bull or even Hyperbull may better reflect your actual beliefs, but be aware you're choosing the more aggressive end of a wide uncertainty range, not a consensus estimate.
- **In all cases**: look at more than one scenario before making any decision based on the output.

## How Scenario Choice Changes the Shape of a Result, Not Just the Size

It's worth noticing that CAGR compounds exponentially with time, which means the gap between scenarios widens dramatically — not linearly — as your chosen time horizon extends. Over a 1-year horizon, the dollar difference between Bear (15%) and Hyperbull (55%) on a given contribution is relatively modest, because compounding hasn't had much time to separate the two paths. Over a 20 or 30-year horizon, that same percentage gap compounds into a dramatically larger absolute dollar difference, simply because exponential growth curves diverge more and more the further out you project them. This is a mathematical property of compounding generally, not something specific to these four scenarios — but it's worth understanding so that a Hyperbull projection at a 30-year horizon isn't mistaken for a modest, conservative-feeling number just because the CAGR itself (55%) sounds like a single, bounded figure.

## Scenario Selection Isn't a One-Time Decision

Because these are adjustable presets rather than fixed outputs, it's reasonable to revisit your selected scenario periodically rather than treating your first choice as permanent. If your own view on Bitcoin's prospects changes — based on new information, a changed risk tolerance, or simply more experience watching the asset's behavior over time — updating which scenario you use for planning purposes is a normal and healthy part of using a projection tool like this one, not a sign that your earlier analysis was wrong.

## Why These Specific Scenarios Aren't Updated Daily

Some tools attempt to continuously recalibrate their growth assumptions based on recent price action — raising projections after a rally, lowering them after a crash. This calculator deliberately doesn't do that, for a specific reason: constantly moving the goalposts based on recent short-term performance tends to produce exactly the wrong behavioral incentive, encouraging more optimistic assumptions right when an asset is most expensive (after a rally) and more pessimistic assumptions right when it's most historically attractive on a relative basis (after a crash). Keeping these four reference points fixed, regardless of recent price action, is a deliberate choice to avoid feeding that specific bias into your own planning.
