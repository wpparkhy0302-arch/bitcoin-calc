## A Pattern Observed Four Times, Not a Law of Physics

Before looking at the history, it's worth stating the limitation up front: Bitcoin has only experienced four halvings so far. Four data points is enough to notice a pattern, but it is a small sample size by any serious statistical standard, and treating it as an immutable law rather than an observed historical tendency is a common overstatement in halving-cycle commentary. With that caveat in place, the pattern itself is genuinely interesting and worth understanding in detail.

## Cycle 1: The November 2012 Halving

The first halving cut the block reward from 50 to 25 BTC. Bitcoin was still an extremely niche asset at this point, trading in the low single digits to low double digits of dollars in the months around the halving. Over the following year, the price rallied dramatically, reaching roughly $1,100 by late November 2013 — an increase on the order of 100x from pre-halving levels, though starting from such a tiny base that the percentage figures from this earliest cycle are not usefully comparable to later, much larger cycles.

## Cycle 2: The July 2016 Halving

The second halving cut the reward from 25 to 12.5 BTC. Bitcoin was trading around $650-670 at the time of this halving. Over the roughly 17 months that followed, the price climbed to its then-all-time high of approximately $19,700 in December 2017 — an increase of roughly 30x. This cycle is the one most associated in public memory with mainstream "Bitcoin mania," including widespread retail and media attention that hadn't existed during the first cycle. The subsequent decline was equally dramatic: by December 2018, the price had fallen to roughly $3,200, a drop of more than 80% from the peak.

## Cycle 3: The May 2020 Halving

The third halving cut the reward from 12.5 to 6.25 BTC, occurring at a price of roughly $8,500, in the middle of the global COVID-19 pandemic's early economic disruption. Over the following 18 months, the price rose to a new all-time high of approximately $69,000 in November 2021 — an increase of roughly 8x, notably smaller in percentage terms than the previous two cycles, though far larger in absolute dollar terms. This cycle featured a mid-cycle dip in mid-2021 (a roughly 50% pullback from a local high around $64,000 to roughly $29,000) before the final push to the November 2021 peak — a reminder that even within an overall "cycle up" period, severe intra-cycle volatility is normal, not an exception. The subsequent 2022 bear market, worsened by the collapse of several major crypto lending platforms and exchanges including FTX in November 2022, took the price down to roughly $15,500-16,000.

## Cycle 4: The April 2024 Halving

The fourth halving cut the reward from 6.25 to 3.125 BTC, occurring at a price of roughly $63,000-64,000. Notably, this cycle's price behavior differed from prior cycles in one important respect: Bitcoin actually reached a new all-time high (above $73,000) in March 2024, *before* the halving itself, driven substantially by the January 2024 approval of spot Bitcoin ETFs in the United States, which opened the asset to a new category of institutional and retail investors through traditional brokerage accounts. This is a meaningful structural change from earlier cycles, where new all-time highs had consistently come well after the halving rather than before it — a sign that institutional capital flows may be altering the cycle's historical timing pattern.

## The Pattern of Diminishing Percentage Returns

Looking across all four cycles, each successive cycle has produced a smaller percentage gain from pre-halving levels to the cycle's eventual peak than the cycle before it — roughly 100x, then 30x, then 8x. This is broadly consistent with what would be expected from a maturing asset: as market capitalization grows into the hundreds of billions and then trillions of dollars, the same dollar amount of new demand produces a smaller percentage price impact than it would against a much smaller market cap. Whether this diminishing pattern continues, stabilizes, or breaks down entirely in future cycles is unknowable in advance — it's a reasonable extrapolation from observed data, not a guarantee.

## What This History Does and Doesn't Support

This history supports the observation that halvings have, so far, preceded periods of substantial price appreciation with a lag of roughly 12-18 months. It does not support treating any specific percentage gain as a repeatable formula for the next cycle, and it does not account for structural changes (like ETF approval) that may make future cycles behave differently from the four that have occurred so far. ## The Timing Lag, Cycle by Cycle

One detail that gets lost in simplified halving narratives is how long the lag was, each time, between the halving itself and the eventual cycle peak:

| Halving | Approx. Lag to Cycle Peak |
|---|---|
| Nov 2012 | ~12 months (to Nov 2013 peak) |
| Jul 2016 | ~17 months (to Dec 2017 peak) |
| May 2020 | ~18 months (to Nov 2021 peak) |
| Apr 2024 | New high arrived *before* halving (Mar 2024), unlike prior cycles |

Notice that the lag has been fairly consistent across the first three cycles — roughly a year to a year and a half — which is part of why market commentary often references "12-18 months post-halving" as a rough rule of thumb for when a cycle's peak might arrive. The fourth cycle broke this pattern, with a new high occurring before the halving rather than after, which is a meaningful data point suggesting the pattern may be shifting as institutional capital flows (via spot ETFs) change the demand-side dynamics in ways that didn't exist in the first three cycles.

## Drawdowns Were Severe in Every Single Cycle, Including the Up Ones

A detail worth emphasizing because it's easy to forget when looking at a multi-year chart: every cycle, including the overall "up" cycles, contained severe intra-cycle drawdowns that would have felt catastrophic to live through in real time. The 2017 cycle included several 30%+ pullbacks before the final euphoric run to $19,700. The 2020-2021 cycle included a roughly 50% mid-cycle correction (from around $64,000 down to roughly $29,000 in mid-2021) before the market recovered and pushed to a new high later that same year. Anyone looking at a "cycle" in hindsight sees a smooth-looking arc; anyone living through it experiences a much rougher, more uncertain path with no guarantee, in the moment, that the eventual recovery is coming.

## A Caution About Precision in These Figures

The price figures cited throughout this article are widely-cited approximations rounded for readability, not exact tick-by-tick historical records — exact prices vary slightly depending on which exchange's data you reference, and even "the all-time high" can differ by a percent or two between data providers depending on which trading venue and timestamp they use as the source. For any decision that depends on precise historical pricing, cross-reference a dedicated historical price data provider rather than relying on rounded figures from a blog post, including this one.
