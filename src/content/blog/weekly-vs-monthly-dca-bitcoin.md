## The Question Behind the Question

Once someone commits to dollar-cost averaging rather than a lump sum, a smaller but still common question follows: should the fixed purchases happen weekly, biweekly, or monthly? It feels like it should matter a lot — more frequent buying sounds like it should "smooth out" volatility more effectively. The honest answer is that frequency matters much less than people expect, and the two factors that actually matter most — total amount invested and total time invested — are the same regardless of whether you split them into 12 monthly purchases or 52 weekly ones.

## Why More Frequent Buying Doesn't Meaningfully Change Your Average

Statistically, increasing purchase frequency from monthly to weekly is a form of increasing your sample size when averaging into a volatile asset. A larger sample size does reduce the *variance* of your average cost basis slightly — you're less exposed to the specific bad luck of whatever day each monthly purchase happens to land on — but it does not change the *expected value* of your average cost basis. Over a 12-month period, 12 monthly purchases and 52 weekly purchases will tend to land on an average price that's quite close to each other, because both are sampling from the same underlying price path over the same window.

Where frequency does matter more is at shorter horizons with higher volatility. A single monthly purchase that happens to land on an unusually volatile day (a day with a 5–10% price swing, which Bitcoin has experienced many times in its history) carries more "luck" risk than one of four weekly purchases averaging across the same month. The effect shrinks as your total investing horizon grows, because any single purchase becomes a smaller fraction of your overall cost basis the longer you've been buying.

## The Factor That Actually Matters More: Transaction Costs

For most retail investors, the practical deciding factor isn't statistical smoothing — it's fees. Many exchanges and brokers charge a flat or percentage-based fee per transaction. Fifty-two weekly purchases at a flat $1 fee each costs $52/year in fees. Twelve monthly purchases at the same flat fee costs $12/year. That $40 difference is often larger than any statistical smoothing benefit from the higher frequency, especially for smaller recurring amounts. Before choosing a frequency, check your specific platform's fee structure — percentage-based fees scale with purchase size and are frequency-neutral, while flat per-transaction fees heavily favor larger, less frequent purchases.

## Matching Frequency to Cash Flow

The most practical consideration is usually the simplest one: how often does money actually become available to invest? Someone paid monthly has a natural monthly cadence; forcing a weekly DCA schedule on monthly income just means withdrawing from a holding account four or five times instead of investing it once, which adds complexity without adding meaningful benefit. Someone paid biweekly or weekly has more flexibility to match their investment schedule to their paycheck schedule, which has the side benefit of making the habit automatic rather than requiring a separate "move money to invest" step.

## A Simple Framework for Choosing

- **If your fee structure is flat per-transaction**: lean toward lower frequency (monthly) to minimize cumulative fees, unless your contribution amount is large enough that fees are a rounding error.
- **If your fee structure is percentage-based or zero**: frequency becomes mostly a matter of personal preference and cash flow matching, since fees don't meaningfully favor either side.
- **If your investing horizon is short (under 2 years)**: slightly higher frequency provides a small amount of additional smoothing against single-purchase bad luck.
- **If your investing horizon is long (5+ years)**: the frequency choice matters increasingly little, because the number of total purchases is large enough either way that the law of averages has already done most of its work.

## What This Looks Like in Practice

Someone dollar-cost averaging $400/month has roughly the same long-run outcome whether they buy $400 once a month or split it into $100 weekly purchases — the difference shows up mainly in fee drag and in how many individual purchase records they need to track for cost-basis purposes, not in the underlying investment outcome. The "set it and forget it" value of DCA comes from the consistency of the schedule, not from squeezing out an extra fraction of a percent by picking the theoretically optimal frequency.

## What About Daily DCA?

Some platforms support daily recurring purchases, which pushes the frequency question to its logical extreme. Daily DCA provides the smallest theoretical reduction in single-purchase "bad luck" risk compared to weekly or monthly, simply because each individual purchase is a smaller fraction of the total, but it also tends to maximize cumulative transaction costs if your platform charges per-transaction fees — potentially 365 fee events per year instead of 12 or 52. For investors on a percentage-fee or zero-fee platform, daily DCA is a perfectly reasonable choice and mainly comes down to personal preference for how granular the automation feels. For investors on a flat-fee platform, daily frequency is usually the least cost-efficient option of the group, and the smoothing benefit it provides over weekly or monthly is small enough that it rarely justifies the added fee drag.

## Biweekly: The Overlooked Middle Option

Biweekly DCA — aligned with a common biweekly payroll schedule — sits between weekly and monthly in both smoothing effect and fee frequency, and it has a practical advantage that's easy to overlook: it matches how many people actually get paid. Scheduling an investment contribution to land the day after a paycheck arrives removes a common source of friction (having to manually move money from a checking account that hasn't been funded yet), which in practice matters more for consistency than the theoretical smoothing difference between biweekly and monthly ever will.

## The Consistency Argument, Restated

All of the frequency analysis above is secondary to the single most important factor in any DCA strategy: whether the schedule actually gets followed, consistently, for years, including through periods when the price has dropped sharply and continuing to buy feels uncomfortable. A theoretically optimal frequency that gets abandoned after six months during a downturn will underperform a theoretically suboptimal frequency that gets followed consistently for five years. If matching your DCA frequency to your paycheck schedule, or to whatever cadence feels most "automatic" and least likely to be skipped, means sacrificing a small amount of statistical optimality, that trade is almost always worth making.

## A Quick Reference Summary

| Frequency | Smoothing Benefit | Fee Impact (flat-fee platforms) | Best Matched To |
|---|---|---|---|
| Daily | Highest (marginal) | Highest | Percentage-fee or zero-fee platforms only |
| Weekly | High | Moderate-high | Weekly paycheck schedules |
| Bi-Weekly | Moderate | Moderate | Bi-weekly paycheck schedules |
| Monthly | Lower (marginal) | Lowest | Flat-fee platforms, monthly income |

None of these differences are large enough to be the deciding factor in whether a DCA strategy succeeds overall — that outcome depends far more on consistency over years than on which row of this table you pick.
